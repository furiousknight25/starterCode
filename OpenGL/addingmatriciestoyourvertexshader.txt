Q: Explain what is meant by Per-Vertex Shading.
    Per-vertex shading (also called Gouraud shading) calculates the lighting equations in the vertex shader at each vertex of the triangle. The resulting colors are then passed down to the rasterizer, which interpolates the color values across the face of the triangle to color the pixels. It is very fast since there are fewer vertices than pixels, but specular highlights can get distorted or missed completely if the highlight lands in the middle of a triangle.

Q: Explain what is meant by Per-Fragment Shading.
    Per-fragment shading (also called Phong shading) calculates the lighting equations in the fragment shader for every single pixel/fragment. The vertex shader outputs geometric vectors (like the normal and light direction), which the rasterizer interpolates across the triangle. The fragment shader then re-normalizes those vectors and computes the full lighting equation per-fragment. This gives much smoother and more accurate lighting, especially for specular highlights, but it does more work since it runs on every fragment.

 
Modifying Your VBO - Step 1

Your previous Vertex Buffer Object contained vertices and vertex colors. We no longer need the vertex colors but we will need vertex normals!  Modify your VBO appropriately for your triangle to set the correct normal vectors at each vertex.

Q: How do you need to modify the VBO for your triangle and what normal vector(s) do you need for your triangle's vertices?
    Instead of storing position + RGB color for each vertex, we now store position + normal vector (nx, ny, nz). Each vertex is packed as 6 floats: (x, y, z, nx, ny, nz).
    Since our triangle lies flat in the XY plane facing toward the camera along +Z, the surface normal points straight out along the Z axis, so all three vertices use the normal vector (0.0, 0.0, 1.0).

Q: Do you need to modify your VAO for your normals?  If so, why?  If not, why not?
    Yes, we need to update the VAO because it tells OpenGL how to read the vertex data from the VBO. Attribute 0 is still the vec3 position at offset 0, but attribute 1 is now our vec3 normal vector at offset 3 * sizeof(float) (with a stride of 6 * sizeof(float)). We need to call glVertexAttribPointer for attribute 1 so the vertex shader's layout(location = 1) in vec3 in_Normal receives the normal data properly.

 
Setting Appropriate Uniform Variables for the Vertex Shader - Step 2

Your vertex shader will need to process the vertices, the normals, and a light position. You will need to make sure you pass in the following for uniform variables:

    mat4 projMatrix, viewMatrix, modelMatrix, and normalMatrix
    vec4 lightPosWorld

The Vertex Shader will also need to transform the vectors appropriately so that they can be passed as varying variables out to the fragment shader.  In particular, this will mean having the following out variables:

    out vec4 normal
    out vec4 lightDir

Note that I'm using vec4 here since it's easiest to do a matrix-vector multiplication with mat4 matrices.

Your resulting vertex shader to prepare for Per-Fragment shading should look something like this:

#version 330 core

layout(location=0) in vec3 in_Position;
layout(location=1) in vec3 in_Normal;

uniform mat4 projMatrix;
uniform mat4 viewMatrix;
uniform mat4 modelMatrix;
uniform mat4 normalMatrix;

uniform vec4 lightPosWorld;

out vec4 normal;
out vec4 lightDir;

void main(void)
{
  normal = normalize(normalMatrix * vec4(in_Normal, 0.0));

  vec4 vertexWorldPos = modelMatrix * vec4(in_Position, 1.0);
  lightDir = normalize(lightPosWorld - vertexWorldPos);

  gl_Position = projMatrix * viewMatrix * vertexWorldPos;
}

I would rename your new vertex shader to something like "vertexShader_PrepForPerFragment.glsl" so it's well named for now.

Note: Be sure you use the GLSL normalize function correctly. It takes a vector as input and returns a normalized vector. 
Computing the Lambertian Shader per Fragment - Step 3

Your final step is to compute the actual Lambertian computation in your fragment shader.  Your fragment shader should look something like this:

#version 330 core

layout(location=0) out vec4 fragmentColor;

uniform vec3 diffuseComponent;

in vec4 normal;
in vec4 lightDir;

void main(void)
{
  float val = max(0.0, dot(normal, lightDir));
  vec3 diffuseShading = vec3( diffuseComponent.r * val, diffuseComponent.g * val, diffuseComponent.b * val );

  fragmentColor = vec4(diffuseShading, 1.0);
}

Note that you will need to set a uniform variable for the diffuse color of the Lambertian shader and pass it in to the fragment shader:

    uniform vec3 diffuseComponent

When you develop a Blinn-Phong shader, you will also need to pass in the specular and Phong exponent values too.

Q: Where will you place your light so that the triangle is lit as you might expect given it's position?
    We place the light in front of the triangle with a positive Z coordinate (for example, at (3.0, 4.0, 5.0) in world space). Since the triangle faces towards +Z, placing the light in front and slightly above/to the side ensures that the dot product between the surface normal and the light direction is positive (N · L > 0), creating clear lighting across the front face. If the light were behind the triangle (z <= 0), the front face would be in shadow.

Q: What "coordinate space" are the shading calculations in for the shaders you've created so far in this lab?  How do you know they are in that space?
    The shading calculations are in World Space.
    We know this because:
    1. In the vertex shader, vertex coordinates are transformed by the model matrix to world space:
       vec4 vertexWorldPos = modelMatrix * vec4(in_Position, 1.0);
    2. The light position is supplied directly in world coordinates (lightPosWorld), and lightDir is calculated as normalize(lightPosWorld - vertexWorldPos).
    3. The normal is transformed by the normalMatrix (the transpose inverse of modelMatrix), which puts it into world space.
    Since both vectors passed into the dot product in the fragment shader are expressed in world space, the lighting computation happens in world space.
