#### 2. Reading Questions Addressed

##### Q: What is meant by the term diffuse material? How would you describe what a diffuse material is? What is a type of diffuse material in the real world?
**A:** A diffuse (or matte / Lambertian) material is a non-shiny surface that does not produce sharp mirror-like specular reflections. When light strikes a diffuse surface, microscopic irregularities and surface roughness scatter incoming photons in random directions across the hemisphere surrounding the surface normal. Because light is scattered uniformly in all directions regardless of the viewer's orientation, the apparent brightness of an ideal diffuse surface depends primarily on the cosine of the angle between the incoming light vector and the surface normal (Lambert's Cosine Law: $I \propto \mathbf{N} \cdot \mathbf{L}$), rather than the observer's viewing angle. 

Common real-world examples of diffuse materials include blackboard chalk, unglazed terracotta/clay pottery, matte drywall paint, cotton fabric, and raw cardboard.

##### Q: Is there an advantage of using an interval class for your code and how might you refactor your code to use this idea?
**A:** Yes, introducing an `interval` class provides several major software engineering and algorithmic advantages:
1. **Encapsulation and Clarity**: Instead of repeatedly passing loose pairs of `double t_min, double t_max` through every function call in the intersection pipeline, an `interval ray_t` struct cleanly bundles bounds into a cohesive unit.
2. **Domain Invariants and Utility Methods**: The interval class encapsulates helper methods such as `contains(x)` (inclusive check $[min, max]$), `surrounds(x)` (exclusive check $(min, max)$), `clamp(x)`, and `expand(delta)`. This prevents off-by-one and boundary clipping bugs.
3. **Hierarchy and BVH Readiness**: Modern ray tracers rely on Bounding Volume Hierarchies (BVH) with Axis-Aligned Bounding Boxes (AABB), which are defined simply as three 1D intervals: `interval x, y, z`. Having an interval class makes adding AABB collision checks effortless.

**Refactoring**: In our codebase, we created `src/math/interval.h` and refactored `Shape::intersect` to accept `interval ray_t`. `ShapeList` initializes the search interval to `[0.001, infinity)` and tightens `ray_t.max = rec.t` whenever a closer intersection is detected, cleanly keeping track of the closest visible hit.

##### Q: Explain in your own words how anti-aliasing makes the jagged edges go away in your rendered images and why?
**A:** Jagged edges ("jaggies" or spatial aliasing) occur because a digital image is a discrete grid of finite pixels, whereas geometric primitives like spheres and triangles have continuous, infinitely sharp mathematical boundaries. When we cast only a single ray through the exact center of each pixel, the decision is binary: either the ray hits the shape or it hits the background. A pixel that covers 90% of a red sphere and 10% of the blue sky turns 100% red, while its immediate neighbor covering 40% of the sphere and 60% of the sky turns 100% blue. This abrupt step function creates harsh staircase patterns.

Antialiasing solves this through supersampling (Monte Carlo box filtering). By firing multiple rays randomly distributed throughout the 1x1 square area of each pixel and averaging their color contributions, the pixel's final RGB value represents the exact fractional area of the pixel covered by each object. A pixel spanning the silhouette border becomes a smooth blend (e.g. 70% object color + 30% background color). To human vision, this gradient transition eliminates the staircase effect, producing smooth, crisp edges.

##### Q: Explain how you compute the normal vector for the Sphere and how you can use that to shade your renderings. What might you call this shader?
**A:** 
1. **Normal Computation**: For a sphere centered at $\mathbf{C}$ with radius $R$, any intersection point $\mathbf{P}$ on its surface satisfies $|\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{C}| = R$. The outward normal vector points radially outward from the sphere center to the surface point:
   $$\mathbf{N}_{\text{outward}} = \frac{\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{C}}{R} = \frac{\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{C}}{\|\mathbf{P} - \mathbf{C}\|}$$
   This vector has unit length ($|\mathbf{N}| = 1$).
2. **Normal Shading**: Because normal components vary continuously between $[-1.0, 1.0]$, we can linearly map them to the RGB color interval $[0.0, 1.0]$:
   $$\text{Color}(r, g, b) = 0.5 \times (\mathbf{N} + \begin{pmatrix} 1.0 \\ 1.0 \\ 1.0 \end{pmatrix})$$
   - Normals pointing toward $+x$ (right) are colored red.
   - Normals pointing toward $+y$ (up) are colored green.
   - Normals pointing toward $+z$ (viewer) are colored blue.
3. **Shader Name**: This is commonly called a **Normal Shader** or **Normal Mapping Visualization Shader**. It is widely used by technical artists and graphics engineers for geometry debugging and surface validation.

#### 3. Mandatory Antialiasing Question

##### Q: With the antialiasing approach described in Section 9.1 where we sample the square of the pixel, discuss how the number of samples might cover the full "area" of the pixel? In other words, what type of coverage might you get if you had 8 samples versus 16 or 32 or 64 or 100? Think about what it might mean for you to have "enough" coverage of a pixel.
**A:** In Section 9.1, antialiasing uses stratified / pseudo-random Monte Carlo sampling over the continuous unit square $[i - 0.5, i + 0.5] \times [j - 0.5, j + 0.5]$ representing each pixel.

- **Coverage Dynamics Across Sample Counts**:
  - **8 Samples**: 8 randomly placed sample points provide very sparse coverage across the 2D area of a pixel. Large spatial gaps remain unsampled. Along high-contrast geometry silhouettes, the statistical variance is high, resulting in visible graininess, stippling, and edge flickering.
  - **16 Samples**: A 2x increase in sample density. Low-contrast geometric edges and smooth gradients look significantly cleaner. However, high-frequency corners and steep silhouette edges will still exhibit noticeable noise and uneven transitions.
  - **32 Samples**: Provides reasonably balanced coverage across the subpixel area. The standard error drops by $\approx \sqrt{2}$, and silhouette edges become smooth to the naked eye at standard viewing distances.
  - **64 Samples**: Densely populates the subpixel square. Subpixel geometric features (such as thin polygon slivers or grazing sphere silhouettes) are reliably detected. Grain is reduced below perceptual thresholds under normal illumination.
  - **100+ Samples**: Approaching asymptotic convergence for primary visibility. Because Monte Carlo integration error decreases at a rate of $O(1 / \sqrt{N})$, quadrupling the sample count from 25 to 100 only cuts the statistical error in half. Visual differences between 64 and 100 samples are virtually imperceptible on static direct illumination, though 100+ samples become essential once indirect diffuse bounces (path tracing) are introduced.

- **What "Enough" Coverage Means**:
  In rendering, "enough" coverage is reached when the residual Monte Carlo variance (sampling noise) drops below the **Just Noticeable Difference (JND)** of human visual perception and the 8-bit display quantization threshold ($1 / 256 \approx 0.0039$). At that point, firing additional rays yields diminishing visual returns while quadratically increasing render time. For primary edge antialiasing at 200x200, **16 to 32 samples per pixel** strikes the optimal sweet spot between visual fidelity and computational efficiency.
